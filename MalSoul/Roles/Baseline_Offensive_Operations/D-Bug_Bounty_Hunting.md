# Bug Bounty Hunting

## Job Profile Summary

A Bug Bounty Hunter is a cybersecurity professional, often a freelancer or independent researcher, who finds and reports security vulnerabilities in software, applications, or online platforms. They participate in official bug bounty programs run by companies, governments, or platforms such as HackerOne and Bugcrowd. In return for responsible disclosure, they may receive rewards and recognition.

## What Do Bug Bounty Hunters Do?

- **Find vulnerabilities:** Look for security flaws in apps, websites, APIs, and systems covered by a program’s scope, using manual testing, security tools, and creativity.
- **Report issues:** Write clear reports with evidence, steps to reproduce the issue, and suggested fixes. Reports go to the company or platform running the program.
- **Follow responsible disclosure:** Test only targets where they have permission and follow the program’s rules.
- **Earn rewards:** Receive payment, public acknowledgment, or both, often based on the severity of the vulnerability.

## Real-Life Example

A major retailer launches a bug bounty program. Within days, a hunter discovers a vulnerability that could allow hackers to access customer data. The hunter reports it responsibly, the company patches the flaw, and the hunter receives a $5,000 reward. Fixing the issue early helps the company avoid a potential data breach and bad press.

## Key Concepts and Must-Know Terms

- **Responsible disclosure:** Reporting vulnerabilities privately and ethically so an organization can fix them before they become public.
- **Scope:** The systems, domains, and types of testing officially allowed by a program.
- **Proof of Concept (PoC):** Evidence that a vulnerability is real, often with a sample or steps showing how it can be reproduced.
- **Bug bounty platforms:** Services such as HackerOne, Bugcrowd, and Synack, where researchers can find company programs.
- **Common vulnerabilities:** SQL injection, XSS (Cross-Site Scripting), IDOR (Insecure Direct Object Reference), CSRF (Cross-Site Request Forgery), open redirects, and more.
- **Reconnaissance:** Gathering information to map exposed systems before testing.

## Prerequisites

### Education and Background

- A solid foundation in cybersecurity, networking, and operating systems.
- A formal degree is not always required; demonstrated skills matter.
- Hands-on learning through CTFs (Capture the Flag), security labs, and self-study.

### Technical Skills

- Familiarity with web technologies such as HTTP, HTML, JavaScript, and APIs.
- Experience with security tools such as Burp Suite, Nmap, Metasploit, and OWASP ZAP.
- Python or JavaScript skills for automating tests and analyzing results.
- Understanding of vulnerabilities covered by the OWASP Top 10.

### Soft Skills

- Analytical thinking and problem-solving.
- Persistence, patience, and curiosity.
- Clear writing for effective vulnerability reports.

### Certifications and Experience

- Many hunters start through self-study or bug bounty-specific learning paths.
- Optional certifications include OSCP, HTB Certified Bug Bounty Hunter, and CEH.

## Why Bug Bounty Hunting Matters

Bug bounty hunters expand a company’s defenses by bringing in diverse security researchers, who may find vulnerabilities that internal teams missed. Organizations improve their security, while hunters can earn recognition and rewards.

> **Authorization note:** Test only assets included in a program’s scope, and follow its rules for testing and disclosure.
